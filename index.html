Understanding and Analysing a Short Story

1. What makes a successful short story?

A short story is a fictional narrative that usually focuses on one main event, a small number of characters and a central conflict. Unlike a novel, it is relatively short and often takes place over a limited period of time. However, a successful short story can communicate powerful ideas, explore human experiences and make readers think.

A good short story usually contains the following elements:

1. An interesting beginning (Introduction)
The opening introduces the main character, setting and situation. It should catch the reader’s attention and make them want to continue reading.
2. Well-developed characters
The characters are the people who take part in the story. Even in a short story, the main character should have a recognisable personality, feelings, motivations and relationships with others. Readers should be able to understand what the character wants and why.
3. A clear conflict or problem
Most short stories revolve around a central conflict. This could be an internal conflict (a struggle within a character, such as fear, guilt or insecurity) or an external conflict (a problem involving another person, society or the environment).
4. A meaningful plot
The plot is the sequence of events in the story. It usually develops from the introduction to a turning point or climax, followed by a resolution. Each event should contribute to the development of the story.
5. An effective ending
A successful ending gives readers something to think about. It may solve the main conflict, reveal something unexpected or leave certain questions unanswered. Sometimes, the ending changes how we understand the entire story.
6. An important message or theme
A short story often explores universal topics such as friendship, love, identity, discrimination, family, courage or personal growth. The theme is the deeper message or central idea that the writer wants readers to reflect on.

2. How do we analyse a short story?

When analysing a short story, we look beyond what happens in the plot. We try to understand how the writer presents the characters, develops the conflict and communicates ideas and messages.

Use the following questions as a guide:

A. Setting – Where and when does the story take place?

  • Where does the action take place?
  • When does the story happen?
  • Does the setting influence the characters or events?
  • Does the atmosphere create a particular mood?

B. Characters – Who are the people in the story?

  • Who is the main character (protagonist)?
  • What do we know about their age, background and personality?
  • What are their strengths and weaknesses?
  • What motivates their actions?
  • How do they feel throughout the story?
  • Do they change or develop? If so, how?
  • What are their relationships with other characters like?

Remember: Writers do not always describe characters directly. We often learn about their personalities through their actions, thoughts, dialogue and interactions with others. This is called characterisation.

C. Plot – What happens in the story?

  • What happens at the beginning?
  • What is the main problem or conflict?
  • What events move the story forward?
  • Is there a turning point or climax?
  • How is the conflict resolved?
  • Is the ending predictable or surprising?

D. Conflict – What is the main struggle?

There are different types of conflict in literature:

Character vs. character
A disagreement or struggle between two or more people.
Character vs. self
An internal struggle involving emotions, decisions or beliefs.
Character vs. society
A struggle against social expectations, discrimination or injustice.
Character vs. nature
A struggle against natural forces or the environment.

Ask yourself: What causes the conflict, and how does it affect the main character?

E. Point of view – Who tells the story?

The point of view determines whose perspective we experience.

  • First-person narrator: The narrator is part of the story and uses “I” or “we”.
  • Third-person narrator: The narrator tells the story using “he”, “she” or “they”. The narrator may know the thoughts and feelings of several characters or focus mainly on one character.

Ask yourself: How does the narrator influence what we know about the characters and events?

F. Language and literary devices – How does the writer communicate ideas?

Writers use different techniques to make their stories more interesting and meaningful. Some common literary devices include:

  • Dialogue: Conversations between characters. Dialogue can reveal personality, emotions and relationships.
  • Imagery: Descriptive language that helps readers imagine what something looks, sounds, smells, tastes or feels like.
  • Simile: A comparison using “like” or “as”.
  • Metaphor: A direct comparison between two things.
  • Symbolism: When an object, person or event represents a deeper meaning.
  • Repetition: Repeating words or phrases to emphasise an idea or emotion.
  • Foreshadowing: Hints about events that may happen later in the story.

G. Theme and message – What is the story really about?

  • What are the main themes?
  • What does the story teach us about people, relationships or society?
  • What message might the writer want to communicate?
  • Does the story connect to real-life experiences or issues?
  • Has the story changed the way you think about a particular topic?

Remember the difference:

  • Topic: What the story deals with (e.g. friendship, bullying or identity).
  • Theme: The deeper idea explored through that topic (e.g. genuine friendship requires trust and acceptance).
  • Message: What readers might learn or take away from the story.

3. From reading to analysis: remember the golden rule!

A good literary analysis does not simply retell the story. It explains how and why the writer uses particular elements to communicate ideas. Always support your interpretations with examples from the text.

For example:

Simple observationRoger is frightened.
Better analysisRoger is frightened because he does not know what Mrs. Jones intends to do with him. His fear is revealed through his actions and his repeated requests to be released.
Even deeper analysisRoger’s initial fear gradually changes into trust. Mrs. Jones’s unexpected kindness challenges his expectations and gives him an opportunity to experience a positive relationship with an adult.

This is what makes literary analysis interesting: we move from identifying information to interpreting it and explaining its significance.
